#+TITLE: Teaching a piano to ring without samples
#+DATE: 2026-10-13
#+FILETAGS: :music:audio:dsp:rust:piano:
#+DESCRIPTION: How I built a piano with no samples inside it, and the things I got backwards on the way: tuning, hammers, strings and tests.
#+TIMEZONE: America/Port_of_Spain
#+OPTIONS: toc:nil num:nil ^:{}
#+STARTUP: showall inlineimages

Resonata is a piano with no recordings inside it. Every note you hear is
worked out on the spot from a felt hammer, one to three steel strings, a
wooden soundboard and a room. It runs as a plug-in or as a standalone
player, and it is free and open source.

This is the story of the first few days of getting it to sound like
something. It is mostly a story about things I had backwards, and about the
tests and measurements that told me so. I've kept the numbers in, because
I'll want to know later whether I broke something.

* What happens when you press a key

#+CAPTION: One key, from finger to speaker. Dashed lines are things that listen or steer.
[[file:img/signal-flow.svg]]

Each key owns a small group of strings: one in the bass, two through the
tenor, three in the treble. A hammer sends a short pulse into them. They
all meet at the bridge, which is where they push on one another, and the
bridge drives a soundboard. Off to the side, some other strings sit
waiting to sympathise. After that it is just a room and whatever effects
you have switched on.

None of that is new. Julius Smith, Balázs Bank, Vesa Välimäki and others
worked it out long before I came along, and the reading list is at the
end. What I can add is a list of ways to get it wrong.

* A string is a delay line

A struck string is a wave running up and down it. In the computer that
becomes a delay line the length of one trip, with a filter in the loop that
loses a little energy each time round. The pitch comes from the total
delay:

\[ f_0 = \frac{f_s}{D} \]

where $f_s$ is the sample rate and $D$ is the whole loop delay in samples,
including the delay the filters add. That last clause is where I came
unstuck. I had subtracted a fudge of 1.5 samples for the filters. The real
delay of the dispersion cascade was about 7. At C4 the piano played
55 cents flat, which is more than half a semitone, and I only found out
because the fitting tool told me. The fix was to calculate each filter's
exact phase delay and let a bisection find the fractional part. (Plain
iteration just flips between two answers, and adding a fractional term on
top counts it twice and costs another 5 cents. I tried both.) The tuning
test now holds C2, C4 and C7 to within 5 cents.

** Real strings are not harmonic

A stiff string sings slightly sharp as you go up its partials:

\[ f_n = n f_0 \sqrt{1 + B n^2} \]

$B$ is the inharmonicity coefficient and it /rises/ with pitch. Mine ran the
other way round, heavy in the bass and almost nothing in the treble. The
result was a C4 whose partials sat 4 to 9 cents flat of a pure harmonic
series, which no stiff string does, and a C7 that was very nearly a sine
wave. Turning the curve the right way up, and then calibrating the
dispersion filters against the loop's real phase, put the C4 partials 2 to
12 cents sharp, as the theory says they should be.

An outside reviewer spotted this on first look. It's obvious once you know:
a real treble note has audibly stretched partials, and a C7 that is nearly
a pure sine wave has none of them.

* Tuning a piano that is never in tune

Concert pianos are not tuned to equal temperament. The bass is tuned flat
and the treble sharp, to meet the inharmonic partials halfway. That's
called stretch, and piano tuners follow a curve described by Railsback.

#+CAPTION: Tuning offset by register, after the fix. The hollow rings are where C1 and C2 were before.
[[file:img/stretch.svg]]

I had used one coefficient both ways and ended up with C1 at -41 cents and C2
at -28. The model matched its own curve exactly, which is a particularly
irritating kind of bug: the string was right, the curve was wrong. The
deviation at the two ends of a real Railsback curve is similar in size,
roughly 25 to 30 cents, but there are far fewer semitones above A4 than
below it, so the treble side is steeper per semitone. The coefficient is
therefore now 0.20 below A4 and 0.35 above. C1 sits at -23.9 cents and C7
at +21.3.

The same all-octaves check found the top of the piano ringing too long. C6
lasted 2.53 s and C7 1.77 s, and I wanted something closer to half a second
to a second. I lowered the decay floor from 1.2 to 0.9 s and got 2.08 and 1.41
respectively. That is closer, but I don't trust it yet. It's on my list
to compare against a real instrument.

* Where the hammer lands

The first version of the excitation was a pile of hacks. Each one broke
something else, and a regression test that failed loudly was the only way I
caught them.

First I stretched a short hammer burst across the whole loop. For a 26-sample
burst and a 1381-sample bass loop that plays it about fifty times too slowly,
so the spectrum collapsed below 500 Hz and louder notes came out darker. I
dropped that altogether. Next I dropped the burst into an empty loop, which
leaves the output at digital zero until the wave comes round again. At C1
that is about 31 ms of exact silence at the start of every note (at
44.1 kHz), and you can hear it. Then there was the loud-note problem: a white
burst at fortissimo spreads a fixed amount of energy over 22 kHz while the
loop sustains below 1 kHz, so the loud note ended up quieter than the medium
one.

The version that survives injects the hammer's force pulse at a point 1/16 of
the string length ahead of where the bridge reads the string, so the first
tone arrives in under 2 ms even at C1. There is no pre-filled noise.

#+CAPTION: Partial 8 has a node where an eighth-of-the-string hammer lands. It is barely excited, and the model notches it on purpose.
[[file:img/strike-point.svg]]

Where the hammer lands matters in a second way. A hammer that strikes at
$1/8$ of the string leaves every eighth partial almost silent, because those
partials have a node there. The comb filter that does this was fake in my
first attempt: I averaged the signal with a copy of itself delayed by one
sample, which is a mild low-pass, not a notch. It's a proper feed-forward
comb now, with dips at partials 8, 16 and 24 and hollow peaks between.

* The hammer was lying about its age

#+CAPTION: Contact time at a moderate velocity (0.6), before and after the treble rebuild.
[[file:img/contact-time.svg]]

The hammer's felt touches the string for a few milliseconds, and how long
decides which partials get excited. Short contact, bright treble. I had
about 1.7 ms at every pitch. That is a low-pass filter at roughly the same
corner everywhere, so the treble partials from the third upwards were being
driven by flat noise. On top of that, the hardness law was inverted: a
harder hammer rang for /longer/.

Now the contact time shortens by a factor of $2^{-1/2}$ per octave, about
4.8 ms at C1 and 0.59 ms at C7 at a moderate velocity (0.6) and neutral
hardness. The whole law is five lines of Rust:

#+begin_src rust
let v = velocity.clamp(0.0, 1.0);
let h = self.hardness.clamp(0.25, 4.0).sqrt();
let base_ms = 3.0 - 2.2 * v;                    // harder blow, shorter contact
let register = 2f32.powf(-(f32::from(midi) - 60.0) / 24.0);
let ms = (base_ms / h * register).clamp(0.25, 6.0);
#+end_src

Dividing by $\sqrt{h}$ is the other half of the fix. The old law handled
hardness the wrong way round, which is why harder hammers rang longer.

The pulse shape took three tries. A haversine has spectral nulls that ate
whole partials. A Gaussian was too dull. The one I kept is $u e^{1-u}$ with a short fade at
the end, and it has no nulls inside the audible band.

The treble also needed its loop filter replacing. It's now a one-pole filter
solved per note so that partial 8 dies about three times faster than partial
1, while the fundamental still hits the decay time in the table. A test
checks that the fundamental lands within 0.2 % and the ratio within 5 % at
C4, C6 and C7.

* Two strings that disagree

Strike a note with two or three strings and you do not get one decay. You
get a quick fall as they move together, then a long, quiet tail as they
drift apart and cancel at the bridge, which then barely moves. Pianists
call the tail the aftersound.

#+CAPTION: A sketch, not a measurement. Unison strings ring in two stages; the bridge is what couples them.
[[file:img/two-stage-decay.svg]]

My first attempt at this was wrong in an instructive way. I had the strings
subtract a share of their average from themselves, which drained energy from
the in-phase motion, the part that should keep the normal decay. A slowly
beating pair of strings stays near that in-phase state for seconds, so every
note simply died too fast. I only noticed because I rendered the same notes
with the previous build. C4 had gone from 6.57 s to 2.64 s.

The version that works adds a correction instead of taking one away:

\[ y_i \leftarrow y_i + \eta\,(y_i - \bar y), \qquad
   1 + \eta = \rho^{\,1/P - 1}, \qquad P = 1 + 3c \]

Here $\bar y$ is the average of the strings, $\rho$ is the normal loop gain,
and $c$ is the coupling amount. The in-phase motion keeps its ordinary decay
and the out-of-phase part rings up to $P$ times longer. With detuned unisons,
the first partial of C4 now has an aftersound of 12.2 s against 7.3 s with
coupling off. With perfectly tuned strings nothing changes, which is correct
too. (The model is Weinreich's, from 1977.)

In code it is a few lines. First the gain, worked out once when the note
starts, with a ceiling so a silly setting cannot run away:

#+begin_src rust
// Bridge coupling: eta = rho^(1/P - 1) - 1, P = 1 + 3c.
let p = 1.0 + 3.0 * coupling.clamp(0.0, 1.0);
let eta = (rho.max(1e-3).powf(1.0 / p - 1.0) - 1.0).clamp(0.0, 0.05);
#+end_src

Then, every sample, each string's output is pushed away from the mean of
all the strings before it goes back into its own loop:

#+begin_src rust
let mean = sum / n;                        // the bridge's motion
for (s, &v) in strings.iter_mut().zip(&outputs) {
    s.commit_looped(v, v + eta * (v - mean));
}
#+end_src

When the strings agree, $y_i - \bar y$ is zero and nothing happens. When they
disagree, the disagreement gets a little less loss than the agreement does.

* Why it sounded like a synth

At one point I said it sounded like a synth. The reasons turned out to be
small and specific.

Every string in a choir was tuned exactly the same, which is the most digital
thing a piano can do. I added a gentle, deterministic wander per key, about
2.4 cents of tuning and 6 % of hammer strength, the same every time you play
the key. Unison strings were also detuned by a fixed number of cents, so the
beating got faster with pitch. At C7 it was 3.6 Hz, which sounds like a
supersaw through a chorus pedal. The detune is now a constant beating rate of
about 0.5 Hz. The last fix was the dryness: I nudged the room and sympathy
defaults up (room 0.15 to 0.22, sympathy 0.5 to 0.6), because a piano with no
bloom reads as a synth.

I also found, while chasing a different issue, that the loop gain was worked
out from the length of the delay line rather than the loop period. That
miscounted the number of times the wave went round, and made decays at C7 27 %
too long. It's the kind of bug you don't hear as a bug. You hear it as
'hm, something is off about the top'.

* One engine, seven instruments

The same machinery turned out to be most of a harpsichord, a clavichord, a
fortepiano and a harp, with different excitations and a few extra stops.

- *Harpsichord:* a quill pluck rather than a hammer, with a buff (lute) stop.
- *Clavichord:* a brass tangent that stays on the string, so pressing harder
  after the note starts raises the pitch. That's called bebung. At full
  pressure it bends the note up about 25 cents.
- *Fortepiano:* light leather hammers, a moderator and a bassoon stop that
  rattles.
- *Concert harp:* finger-plucked strings that ring until you stop them, with
  pinch harmonics and a glissando pedal.

The two electric pianos needed new physics. The tine is a clamped-free beam
whose modes sit in the ratio 1 : 6.267 : 17.547, plus a tone bar, read by a
magnetic pickup. The reed is struck steel read by an electrostatic one. They
had a start-up spike that took the output to 16, because I was
differentiating a flux that began from zero rather than from rest. Starting the
flux at its rest value fixed it.

All ten pedals work on all of them, and any four can sit on the on-screen
pedals. The pedals I invented (super sostenuto, glissando) are defined in
the manual, because there is no standard to follow.

* Real time, and other things you only learn the hard way

The audio thread isn't allowed to wait for anything. No locks, no
allocation, no files. I got this wrong, or something close to it, in three
places, and found out about each one by accident.

- The standalone player shared its engine behind a lock and built a vector in
  every callback. It now owns the engine outright and receives MIDI through a
  ring buffer.
- The plug-in's smoothing did nothing. It read the raw value and then ran the
  smoothers into a buffer it threw away. Every knob move was a step, so you
  would have heard zipper noise on every parameter. Parameters are now smoothed
  per 64-sample block.
- The meter decayed up to 64 times too slowly, because a per-sample factor was
  being applied once per block.

The editor had its own bug. The meter and lamps only moved while the cursor
was over the window. The graphics toolkit draws a frame only when an event
wakes it, and the audio thread's polite request to redraw is only looked at
just before drawing, which nobody triggers. A 15 frames-per-second timer
fixes it.

The sound has its own budget. A worst-case pile-up (the full 32 voices, with
the sustain pedal down and every effect on) runs at about 11 times real time
on a two-core cloud machine. SIMD bought most of that: groups of strings in
one instruction, and the soundboard modes in two banks of eight. The
unoptimised version of that test is far slower, so the speed check only runs
on release builds.

* The tests argued back

Some of my tests were wrong, or failed for the wrong reason, and those taught me the most.

- A test of treble partial sharpness passed for weeks. I later found it was
  passing on noise: the old sustain had partials 3 to 6 all at about -7 dB, the
  signature of flat noise, and a flat spectrum has something near every
  expected frequency. Once the noise went, the test had to ask for real
  structure.
- The golden-file test compares a render against a stored one. It flagged
  clipping that a mono peak hid: the left channel reached +1.05 while the
  right sat at +0.59, which averages to a comfortable 0.82.
- Measuring late in the note, not early, was the only way to see anything
  about pedals. The release transients are loud in every case and drown the
  effect.
- Tests of the tools mattered as much as tests of the piano. A parabolic
  peak-picker with a sign error put every B measurement out by 9
  cents, and I'd have been tuning the piano to fit a broken ruler.
- When I put the plug-in through a validator, it found two places where a
  clamp had its limits the wrong way round at very low sample rates, and a
  phaser that went unstable once its frequency passed half the sample rate.
  It now survives anything from 1 kHz to 768 kHz.

* Things that fooled me

A few measurements fooled me for longer than they should have.

At C6 the analysis showed a fundamental 19 dB down, a hole that was not there.
I had told the analyser to expect a perfectly tuned string, but with the
piano's natural stretch a comb mode of the room at 1046.2 Hz sat 0.3 Hz from
my prediction and stole the slot. The string was fine.

A bump at 2.7 to 3 kHz looked like a resonance. It was two nearly equal
partials, one longitudinal and one transverse, 3 to 4 % apart, that a
peak-picker kept confusing. The soundboard was innocent.

After one rebuild the treble measured identical to 0.1 dB. That was not a
subtle bug: the plug-in in the host had not been rescanned, so I was
listening to the old binary.

Then there was my own playing. I compared a recording of it with a reference
recording and the numbers were miles apart: centroid 5251 Hz against 730 Hz.
I assumed the synth. I rendered a single note and measured it the same way,
and it sat with the reference. The difference was me: playing about 1.4 times
too fast, nothing below E4, one volume throughout, in a dry, narrow
recording. I changed no code.

* The rest of the box

There's more in there than I have the room to explain properly, so here is
the short version.

- *Effects:* tremolo, wah, chorus, flanger, phaser, fuzz, delay, amp
  simulation, compressor and two equalisers. Each is bit-for-bit transparent
  when off.
- *Rooms:* a light built-in reverb, and a convolution mode with four
  synthesised spaces or an impulse response of your own. It adds no latency.
- *Spatial:* first-order Ambisonics output and a decode to a 7.1.2 bed.
- *Tuning:* temperaments, Scala files and stretch.
- *Tools:* an analyser for recordings, a fitter that learns voicing from a
  recording, and a blind-test page generator.
- *The fitter:* the engine has hard switches in it (felt, dampers), so I
  couldn't differentiate it. It estimates gradients by perturbing every
  parameter at once and rendering twice, a method called SPSA. On a test
  target it brings the spectral distance down by 20 to 50 % in 40 steps.

* How it holds up

| What                                   | Result                              |
|----------------------------------------+-------------------------------------|
| Worst-case pile-up, every effect on    | 11.2 times real time (2-core x86)   |
| clap-validator                         | 36 passed, 0 failed, 8 skipped      |
| Sample rates                           | 32 to 96 kHz sound alike            |
| Survives without crashing              | 1 kHz to 768 kHz                    |
| Single C1 at full velocity             | peaks at 0.77 of full scale         |

That last row is a trim of 3 dB that I added at the end. A low note at full
velocity used to go just past full scale before any effects.

#+begin_src rust
/// Fixed -3 dB master trim, 10^(-3/20), applied with the Volume control.
pub const OUTPUT_TRIM: f32 = 0.707_945_8;
// ...
let vol = self.params.volume * OUTPUT_TRIM;
#+end_src

* Reading

If you only read five things:

- Julius O. Smith, /Physical Audio Signal Processing/.
- Bank, Välimäki and colleagues on piano modelling: commuted synthesis,
  soundboards, dispersion.
