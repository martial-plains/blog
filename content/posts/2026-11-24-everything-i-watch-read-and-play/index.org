#+DESCRIPTION: How I keep every film, show, book and game I've watched, read or played as an Org note: importing years of history, filling in the gaps, finding things again, and keeping it in step with a media server.

Every film I've watched, every show I've started, every book I've read and every
game I own has its own Org note. There are 1,822 of them. They sit in one folder
next to the rest of my notes, and Emacs is what I use to add to them, search
them and keep them current.

I wrote about [[file:../2026-11-03-why-org-is-where-all-my-notes-live/index.org][why my notes live in Org]] three weeks ago. This is one corner
of that, and the one with the most code in it.

* Why not just use the apps

For years the record lived in other people's databases. Trakt knew what I
watched, Goodreads knew what I read, and Steam knew what I owned. Each of them
is good at its own thing. None of them knows about the others, and none of them
is a place for my own writing.

I wanted three things the apps don't give me together:

- *One place for all of it*, so I can ask "what were my best crime stories this
  year?" and get books and shows in the same answer.
- *Room to write.* A review, a quote, a note about who recommended it, or a whole
  guide to a game, in the same file as the title.
- *A record that outlives the service.* A folder of plain text files will still
  open when an app closes or changes its export format.

Org already does the third one by being Org. The first two needed some work.

The third one also stopped being hypothetical. After Trakt's last few updates I
mostly stopped using it, and I now track everything in Org. Trakt is where the
history came from. From here on, the notes are the record.

* One note per title

Each title is a file with one top-level heading. Everything a program needs to
know about it lives in that heading's property drawer, and everything I write
lives under the subheadings. Here is a show I'm in the middle of, trimmed:

#+begin_src org
,* Boston Legal (2004)                                   :show:comedy:drama:
:PROPERTIES:
:TYPE: show
:TITLE: Boston Legal
:YEAR: 2004
:STATUS: watching
:RATING: 9
:RATED: 2026-09-15
:EPISODES_WATCHED: 87
:AIRED_EPISODES: 101
:PROGRESS: 86%
:LAST_WATCHED: 2026-11-14
:GENRES: Comedy, Drama
:POSTER: https://image.tmdb.org/t/p/w342/b0bXUCfpcsgQ6EVVmucU2G76ixT.jpg
:IMDB: tt0402711
:TMDB: 4598
:TVDB: 74058
:END:
,** Overview
,** Review
,** Notes
,** Seasons
,*** Season 1
| Ep | Title                             |    Watched | Rating |
|----+-----------------------------------+------------+--------|
|  1 | Head Cases                        | 2026-08-31 |        |
|  2 | Still Crazy After All These Years | 2026-08-31 |        |
#+end_src

The four kinds share the core properties
(=TYPE=, =TITLE=, =YEAR=, =STATUS=, =RATING=, =GENRES=, =POSTER=) and then add
their own. A film has =PLAYS= and a watch log with a timestamp for each viewing.
A show has a table per season with every episode, the dates I watched it and an
optional rating. A book has =AUTHOR=, =PAGES=, =STARTED=, =FINISHED= and a
reading log. A game has =PLATFORM= and =HOURS=, and gets a folder of its own
instead of a single file, because some games come with guides. Hay Day has
seventeen notes and a copy of its wiki beside its main one.

Genres are copied into the heading's tags as well (=:comedy:drama:=). That
duplicates information, and it's deliberate: it means plain Org tag searches
work on my media without any of my code loaded.

The ratings run from 1 to 10, the same scale Trakt uses, so nothing had to be
converted on the way in. The status words depend on the type: films are
/watched/ or /plan-to-watch/, books go /to-read/, /reading/, /read/ or /dnf/,
and games go /backlog/, /playing/, /completed/, /100%/ or /dropped/.

* Getting years of history in

Nobody types 1,822 notes. The history came in through four imports, each
an =M-x= command that reads an export or an API and writes notes in the format
above.

| Source    | What came in                                              |
|-----------+-----------------------------------------------------------|
| Trakt     | 638 watched films, 479 shows, 22,939 plays, 1,798 ratings |
| Goodreads | 132 books, 92 star ratings, 117 finish dates              |
| Steam     | 301 games, with playtime                                  |
| Jellyfin  | what's in progress on my media server, and where I paused |

Every import has to be safe to run twice. I ran them more than once while I was
filling in the gaps, the Steam import is meant to be re-run to pick up new games
and playtime, and a second run must never clobber a review I wrote in between.
So each importer owns a fixed set of properties and sections and leaves
everything else alone: my /Overview/, /Review/ and /Notes/, any headings I've
added, my tags. Ratings, plays and the episode tables are the importer's. My
words are mine.

Each source had its own quirk.

*Trakt only knows what I told it.* I joined in June 2025, and for most of the
history from before that, Trakt has the release or air date rather than the day
I watched. The import faithfully copies that. The result is a table that says I
watched 76 episodes of television in 2000. I didn't; that's when those episodes
aired. The index now says so above the table, because the data can't be fixed
after the fact and I'd rather have a caveat than a false precision.

*Goodreads exports titles but not authors or ISBNs.* The import brought in my
shelves, ratings and dates, and a second command fills in the rest from Open
Library: authors, page counts, covers, genres and ISBNs. It only accepts a match
whose title looks like the same book, and it lists the ones it skipped so I can
give them an ISBN by hand.

*Steam's playtime and mine are different numbers.* Some games I've played
somewhere else too. So the note keeps Steam's figure in =STEAM_HOURS= and my
total in =HOURS=, and an import only ever raises =HOURS= to Steam's figure,
never lowers it. Sessions I log by hand with =C-c e g= survive.

Steam was also the most honest import. Of 301 games, 285 are in the backlog. 137
have any playtime at all.

* Filling in the gaps

An export tells you that you watched something. It doesn't tell you what the
thing was. Three more commands fill that in:

- *TMDB* adds genres, a poster, an overview, the runtime, the director or
  creators, and for shows the network and whether it has ended.
- *Open Library* does the same for books, as above.
- *MyAnimeList* links for anime and manga. Anime are matched by the TVDB and TMDB
  ids Trakt already gave me, through a community-kept mapping of ids, so there's
  no guessing by title. MAL lists each season as its own entry, so a show gets a
  link per season.

Each of these only touches notes that haven't been enriched yet, and each writes
the date it ran into =ENRICHED=, so running one again is quick and does nothing
surprising. 1,467 of the 1,822 notes now have genres; the rest are mostly
obscure games and books with no match.

Posters are linked, not downloaded. Each note starts its /Overview/ with an
image link to TMDB, Open Library or Steam, and Org can show remote images inline
if you ask it to:

#+begin_src emacs-lisp
(setq org-display-remote-inline-images 'cache) ; fetch https images, keep them
(setq org-image-actual-width nil)              ; honour #+attr_org: :width 250
#+end_src

The folder stays small enough to sync anywhere, and a note opens with its poster
at the top. For a book I add by hand, giving an ISBN when I create the note is
enough to get its cover.

* Finding things again

A pile of notes is only useful if you can ask it questions. Most of the everyday
work is on =C-c e=:

| Keys      | Does                                                    |
|-----------+---------------------------------------------------------|
| =C-c e n= | new book, game, film or show                            |
| =C-c e f= | filter by type, genre, rating and status                |
| =C-c e r= | best finished titles in a genre, to send someone        |
| =C-c e s= | rate or re-rate a title, 1 to 10                        |
| =C-c e d= | finish something: status, date, rating, optional review |
| =C-c e b= | log reading progress                                    |
| =C-c e g= | log a play session                                      |
| =C-c e v= | write a dated review                                    |
| =C-c e u= | finished titles I haven't rated yet                     |

The piece I use most, though, isn't a command. It's a dynamic block. Org lets
you put a =#+BEGIN:= line anywhere, and =C-c C-c= on it calls a function named
after the block to fill in what's between that line and =#+END:=. So a list like
this can live in any note, and update itself on request:

#+begin_src org
,#+BEGIN: ent-list :types ("movie") :tags ("horror") :rating "8+, !10" :limit 5
| Title                 | Type  | Rating | Genres                           | Status  |
|-----------------------+-------+--------+----------------------------------+---------|
| Backrooms (2026)      | movie |      9 | Horror, Mystery, Science Fiction | watched |
| Cure (1997)           | movie |      9 | Horror, Mystery, Crime, Thriller | watched |
| Hell House LLC (2015) | movie |      9 | Horror, Mystery                  | watched |
| Heretic (2024)        | movie |      9 | Thriller, Horror                 | watched |
| Jigsaw (2017)         | movie |      9 | Horror, Mystery                  | watched |
,#+END:
#+end_src

The titles are links to their notes; I've left the link markup out here. The
function behind it is short, because Org does the awkward part: it parses
everything after the block's name into a property list, so =:types= arrives as
the list ="movie"= and =:limit= arrives as the number 5.

#+begin_src emacs-lisp
(defun org-dblock-write:ent-list (params)
  "Fill an ent-list block with a table of the titles that match PARAMS."
  (let ((dir (file-name-directory (buffer-file-name))))
    (insert "| Title | Type | Rating | Genres | Status |\n|-\n")
    (dolist (it (ent-query (ent-items) params))
      (insert (format "| [[file:%s][%s]] | %s | %s | %s | %s |\n"
                      (file-relative-name (plist-get it :file) dir)
                      (plist-get it :title)
                      (ent-prop it "TYPE") (ent-prop it "RATING")
                      (ent-prop it "GENRES") (ent-prop it "STATUS"))))
    (delete-char -1)
    (org-table-align)))
#+end_src

=ent-items= returns one property list per note, and =ent-query= filters and
sorts them. My hubs are made of these blocks: one for what I'm watching, one for
what I've finished but not rated, one for the backlog, sorted by when I added
each title.

The =:rating= parameter has a small language of its own, because "8 and up"
wasn't enough for long. A term can be a number, a range
(=5-7=), =8+=, =>=8=, =<=6=, =>7=, =<5= or =unrated=, and a =!= in front leaves
it out. So ="8+, !10"= is "good but not perfect", and ="!unrated"= means "only
things I've rated". Each term becomes a range:

#+begin_src emacs-lisp
(defun ent-rating-range (term)
  "Read one rating TERM as a (LOW . HIGH) range, or nil if it can't be read.
Unrated titles count as 0, so \"<=6\" leaves them out."
  (cond
   ((member term '("unrated" "none")) (cons 0.0 0.0))
   ((string-match "\\`>=\\(.+\\)" term)
    (let ((n (ent--num (match-string 1 term)))) (and n (cons n 10.0))))
   ((string-match "\\`<=\\(.+\\)" term)
    (let ((n (ent--num (match-string 1 term)))) (and n (cons 0.01 n))))
   ((string-match "\\`>\\(.+\\)" term)
    (let ((n (ent--num (match-string 1 term)))) (and n (cons (+ n 0.01) 10.0))))
   ((string-match "\\`<\\(.+\\)" term)
    (let ((n (ent--num (match-string 1 term)))) (and n (cons 0.01 (- n 0.01)))))
   ((string-match "\\`\\(.+\\)\\+\\'" term)
    (let ((n (ent--num (match-string 1 term)))) (and n (cons n 10.0))))
   ((string-match "\\`\\([^-]+\\)-\\([^-]+\\)\\'" term)
    (let ((a (ent--num (match-string 1 term)))
          (b (ent--num (match-string 2 term))))
      (and a b (cons a b))))
   (t (let ((n (ent--num term))) (and n (cons n n))))))
#+end_src

The one decision in there worth pointing out is that an unrated title counts
as 0. Without that, ="<=6"= would quietly sweep in every film I haven't got
round to rating, and a list of "things I didn't like" would be mostly things I
hadn't thought about.

One thing the snippet hides: reading 1,822 files with Org every time a block
updates is slow, several seconds. The real =ent-items= keeps an index in memory,
builds it once, and updates a single entry from an =after-save-hook= whenever I
save a note under =resources/media/=.

And if none of my code is loaded, the tags still work. With the media folder
in =org-agenda-files=, a tags search such as =+horror+RATING>=8= in the agenda
finds the same films.

* Keeping it in step with the media server

The imports bring history in. They don't keep anything current. What I watch
plays through Jellyfin, my media server, so the last piece is a two-way sync
between the notes and the server: if I watch something there, the note gets a
watch-log line, and if I mark something watched in a note, the server marks it
played.

Two-way sync is easy to get subtly wrong, so the rules are short and written at
the top of the code:

- *Watched on one side only:* the other side catches up.
- *Watched on both:* the newest last-watched date wins. The note keeps every date
  it has ever seen; the server only keeps the latest.
- *Un-watching is never pushed.* If something was watched at the last sync and one
  side now says it isn't, the sync lists it under "Left alone" and changes
  nothing.
- *Only ids push.* Titles are matched by TMDB, TVDB, IMDb or MyAnimeList id. A
  title that only matches by name and year is pulled into the note, and its ids
  are copied across, but nothing is pushed to the server for it until the next
  run, when it will match by id.

The third rule is the one that needs memory. Without knowing what the last run
saw, "watched in the note, unwatched on the server" looks the same whether I
just watched it or I deliberately cleared it on the server. So the sync keeps a
small state file of what each title looked like last time (369 entries for
Jellyfin at the moment). For a film, the whole decision comes down to this:

#+begin_src emacs-lisp
(defun msync-plan-movie (note server prev)
  "What to do for one movie: (SIDE . WHY), or nil for nothing.
NOTE and SERVER are plists with :watched (t or nil) and :last (a
YYYY-MM-DD string or nil). PREV is what the last sync recorded."
  (let ((nw (plist-get note :watched))
        (sw (plist-get server :watched))
        (nl (or (plist-get note :last) ""))
        (sl (or (plist-get server :last) "")))
    (cond
     ;; Watched at the last sync, unwatched on one side now: hands off.
     ((and (plist-get prev :played) (not (eq nw sw)))
      '(left-alone . "unwatched on one side since the last sync"))
     ((and sw (not nw)) '(note . "watched on the server: add it to the watch log"))
     ((and nw (not sw)) '(server . "watched in the note: mark it played"))
     ((and nw sw (string> nl sl)) '(server . "the note is newer: update the server"))
     ((and nw sw (string> sl nl)) '(note . "the server saw it again: add the date")))))
#+end_src

Shows run the same rule per episode. Dates are =YYYY-MM-DD= strings, so
comparing them as strings compares them as dates.

The function returns a decision rather than doing anything, and that's the
important part. =C-c m s= runs every decision, shows the whole plan in a buffer,
and only then asks whether to apply it. A sync that can rewrite hundreds of
notes should show its working first. It also runs in the background, so I can
keep typing while it talks to the server.

The same code knows Emby, Plex and MyAnimeList, each with its own limits. Plex
can't be given a past date, so anything pushed there is stamped "now". Jellyfin
only remembers the latest play of each title, which is why the note, not the
server, is the record of every viewing.

* What 1,822 notes say about me

Once everything was in one place, a stats page was a few lines of code away.
Some of it was a surprise.

- *I rate almost everything a 6, 7 or 8.* Of 1,169 rated titles, 895 land there.
  Only 100 get a 9 or 10, and 21 get below a 4. A 1-to-10 scale is really a
  6-to-8 scale with a few exceptions.
- *I rate a lot and write very little.* 1,169 ratings, and reviews for three
  titles. The structure is there for every note, a dated /Review/ heading
  waiting to be filled. It mostly stays empty.
- *2020 was my reading year.* I finished 84 books that year and haven't finished
  more than 16 in any year since.
- *I start more than I finish.* 490 shows started, 357 completed, and 122 on hold,
  which here means I haven't watched an episode in sixty days.

None of that needed the notes to be in Org. But I'd never have asked the
questions while the answers were spread over four websites.

* The honest downsides

*The imports are most of the work.* The commands that use the notes are the easy
part. Getting four services' data in cleanly, matching titles between them, and
making every import safe to re-run is where the code and the edge cases are.

*Matching is never perfect.* Name-and-year matches are wrong often enough that I
don't trust them to push anything. Some books have no match on Open Library at
all, and a few games will never have genres.

*It's my data, so it's my job.* Now that I've mostly left Trakt, nothing records
what I watch unless it plays through Jellyfin or I log it myself. If I watch
something elsewhere and forget, the notes simply don't know. Trakt's scrobbling
used to catch those for me.

*It's a lot of files.* 1,822 notes, plus a game's guides, plus the hubs. Org
handles it fine, but an index that's kept up to date in memory is not optional
at this size.

* What I took away

*Separate the importer's fields from yours.* Every import is safe to re-run
because each one owns a known set of properties and never touches the rest. That
single rule made it possible to keep re-importing without fear.

*A sync should decide first and act second.* Returning a plan instead of making
changes is what makes the preview possible, and the preview is what makes the
sync trustworthy.

*Never infer intent from absence.* "Not watched" on one side might mean "not
yet" or "deliberately cleared". The sync can't tell, so it doesn't guess, and it
says so.

*Duplicate on purpose for the plain tools.* Putting genres in the tags as well
as the properties costs a few bytes per note and means Org's own agenda can
search my media with none of my code.

And the one I keep relearning: a record you can write in gets written in. Mostly
ratings, so far. Three reviews is a start.
